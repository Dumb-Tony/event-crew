## Product definition

EVENT CREW is a 2–5 player cooperative work-comedy game about building weddings, concerts, festivals, conferences, parties, and sporting events before the public arrives. The clock never negotiates. Players unload, assemble, route power, decorate, test, repair, and improvise; when the deadline reaches zero, the event begins in the condition they created rather than cutting to a binary win/lose screen.

The player fantasy is being a capable, under-equipped crew who turns a pile of cases and a client brief into a working event—then survives the consequences of every shortcut. The humor is systemic: a table blocks a guest route, the reroute snags a cable, the disconnected speaker hides the vows, and the client still demands photographs.

Target audience: co-op players who enjoy *Overcooked*-style readability, job-sim tactility, and stories generated by mistakes. A browser job lasts 3–5 minutes. The eventual Unity game targets 15–30 minute contracts, with 1–3 contracts per session and support for solo plus 2–5 player online co-op.

## Design pillars

1. **The deadline is a world event, not a fail screen.** Guests arrive and shows start regardless of readiness.
2. **Work is physical and interdependent.** Placement, access, power, fragile gear, weather, and human traffic combine into legible consequences.
3. **Simple verbs, difficult coordination.** Move, carry, place, connect, operate, inspect. Depth comes from sequence and shared space.
4. **Readable chaos.** Every escalation has a visible cause, warning, consequence, and recovery path.
5. **The town remembers.** Jobs affect reputation, repeat clients, venue condition, and later work when that adds meaningful continuity.

## Core gameplay loop

Accept a contract → read the brief and site hazards → stage deliveries → assemble and place dependencies → connect power and systems → test and verify requirements → handle disruptions → deadline triggers the live event → work around guests/performers → receive a graded debrief → repair, buy upgrades, and select the next job.

Moment to moment, players scan for the next dependency, navigate crowded paths, pick up one object or operate one tool, communicate intent, place or connect it, observe feedback, and reprioritize. Heavy items later require two players; rolling cases move quickly but build momentum; powered systems create cable obstacles and circuit load.

## Systemic simulation

- **Layout:** snap zones express requirements, but free placement remains possible. Items obstruct crew and crowd flow. Some assets require clearances or precedents.
- **Power:** devices draw amps from named circuits. Overload trips a breaker; players must isolate loads and reset it. Later: cable length, splitters, generators, wet connections, and phases.
- **Schedule:** deliveries, vendors, rehearsals, guests, and show cues occur at fixed times. Setup continues into the live phase.
- **Crowd flow:** agents choose routes, queue, detour around blockers, occupy chairs, and interact with incomplete spaces.
- **Fragility:** future items accumulate impact and weather damage. Broken items may remain usable with degraded output.
- **Weather:** wind moves light decor, rain threatens power and fabric, heat affects guests and generators. Forecasts are imperfect but legible.
- **Requirements:** explicit client checks coexist with hidden quality measures such as symmetry, sightlines, trip hazards, cleanliness, and noise.
- **Causality standard:** hazards telegraph before failure; radio, animation, sound, and UI identify cause; at least one recovery action remains.

## Tools, vehicles, and jobs

Starter tools: work gloves, dolly, tape, cable ramps, level, multi-tool, circuit tester, ladder, radio, checklist clipboard. Vehicles: panel van, box truck, utility cart, scissor lift, forklift, generator trailer. Equipment can be improved for capacity, durability, handling, and diagnostic clarity rather than raw speed alone.

Job families:

- Weddings: seating, tables, arches, catering power, lighting, sound, weather plan.
- Concerts/festivals: stage decks, barricades, backline, generators, crowd gates, changeovers.
- Conferences: pipe-and-drape, registration, breakout-room resets, projection, network and audio.
- Parties: themed decor, inflatables, catering, neighbor/noise constraints.
- Sports: field dressing, temporary seating, scoreboards, concessions, safety lanes.
- Emergency changes: room flips, vendor no-shows, storms, equipment swaps, surprise attendance.

## Progression, economy, and world

Contracts pay a base fee plus requirement, safety, speed, and client bonuses. Damage, rentals, overtime, and refunds reduce profit. Reputation unlocks prestigious/riskier contracts and trusted vendors. Money buys tools, vehicle capacity, spare inventory, better forecasting, and a larger yard. Upgrades should create strategic options—not erase coordination.

The shared setting is **Rookery County**, a slightly underfunded small-town region usable by all five work-game concepts. Recurring places include Hawthorn Hall, Foundry Park, the civic center, county fairgrounds, marina, and high school stadium. Recurring clients, public-works crews, vendors, radio hosts, and residents remember prior disasters. Persistent changes should be selective: venue damage, community reputation, supplier relationships, and major town events—not exhaustive world simulation.

## Multiplayer roles and coordination

No locked classes. Players fall into temporary roles based on tools and information: runner/stager, layout lead, power/audio tech, assembler, and troubleshooter. Shared jobs encourage handoffs, parallel work, two-person carries, call-and-response testing, and negotiated use of narrow routes. Solo play uses lighter objects, optional task pinning, and eventually a simple “hold this / bring that” crew helper. Co-op authority should be symmetric; the checklist is shared.

Network design for Unity: host-authoritative job state and physics ownership; clients predict locomotion and carried-object motion; discrete interactions use request/confirm; requirement evaluation runs on the host. Late join restores a compact job snapshot. Avoid precision stacking as a critical success condition because latency turns it punitive.

## Failure and chaos states

There is no early global fail in normal contracts. A blown breaker, blocked aisle, damaged centerpiece, late truck, missing chairs, rain-soaked cable, or incorrect layout changes the live event and score. Players may recover during the live phase, but crowds reduce access. Safety-critical jobs may be shut down locally (one circuit, one entrance, one ride) with clear repair steps. The end grade reports cause and consequence rather than only points.

## Art, audio, interface, and accessibility

Art direction: stylized 3D “municipal catalog” realism—chunky silhouettes, readable materials, safety orange crew accents, faded venue colors, stenciled labels, and grounded but forgiving physics. Equipment should be recognizable at a glance and show state in the world (lit switches, cable runs, leaning stacks). Browser v0.1 uses a top-down canvas version of that visual grammar.

Audio direction: close, tactile work sounds; radio compression for task updates; spatial hums and buzzes for powered systems; a musical mix that shifts from prep percussion to diegetic live-event music at deadline. Comedy comes from timing and contrast, not quips. Critical cues (breaker trip, arrival warning) receive distinct sound plus visual feedback.

UI: world-first with a persistent deadline, compact requirements, circuit meters, contextual interaction prompt, and radio feed. Accessibility baseline: keyboard and button parity where practical, remappable controls in Unity, captions, non-color state markers, scalable UI, reduced motion, independent sound channels, high-contrast interactable outlines, hold/toggle options, and alternatives to rapid tapping. The browser slice is keyboard-first and responsive but does not yet support touch gameplay.

## Replayability and content generation

Jobs combine authored venue layouts with parameterized briefs, delivery order, guest count, weather, circuit availability, vendor timing, equipment faults, and optional demands. Generation must preserve solvability and readable dependencies. Daily contracts can share a seed. Hand-authored “story contracts” introduce systems; procedural variations test mastery. Score categories, alternate layouts, efficiency records, and persistent consequences support replay without turning the game into a pure speedrun.

## First browser vertical slice

**Playable claim:** in under five minutes, one player can understand a wedding brief, move physical equipment from delivery staging into venue zones, manage a constrained power circuit, verify the result, and see guests enter whatever setup exists at the deadline.

Current scope:

- One top-down wedding venue and delivery yard.
- Three-minute real-time setup phase and 22-second live-event phase.
- Move; pick up/drop; connect/disconnect power; reset breaker; inspect checklist.
- Six chairs, two tables, one arch, sound system, decorative lights.
- 15A circuit. Sound draws 8A; lights draw 9A; powering both trips it.
- Fixed placement zones with continuous free movement.
- Requirements, radio feedback, end-state evaluation, retry.
- Guests walk into the player-authored layout when time expires.

Explicit exclusions: networking, physics engine, inventory economy, saves, touch controls, audio, weather, vehicles, damage, multiple venues, AI crew, and procedural jobs. These are deferred to protect a quick satisfying loop.

## Technical approach and state architecture

The prototype is plain HTML/CSS/JavaScript and one Canvas, with no packages or build step. `index.html` owns accessible surrounding UI; `styles.css` owns responsive presentation; `game.js` owns simulation and rendering. It can run by opening the file directly or through any static file server.

State is one serializable object containing phase, clocks, player, items, power flags, guests, verification, and messages. Static world definitions (zones, capacities) sit outside runtime state. The frame loop advances simulation from elapsed time, renders from state, and then projects state into accessible DOM UI. Requirement evaluation is a pure derived query. Next refactor: split input, simulation, rules, render, audio, and scenario data before adding a second job.

Suggested production browser structure:

```text
src/
  game.js              # orchestration and phase machine
  input.js             # remappable action map
  simulation/          # movement, placement, power, crowds
  rules/requirements.js
  render/canvas.js
  ui/hud.js
  data/jobs/*.json
  audio.js
tests/
  requirements.test.js
  power.test.js
  phase.test.js
```

Unity migration: preserve scenario data, requirement predicates, event names, and phase model; replace canvas movement with Rigidbody/CharacterController interaction; use ScriptableObjects for equipment/jobs; isolate deterministic rules from MonoBehaviours; use Unity Input System and accessibility action maps; add Netcode only after a two-player graybox proves carry/placement ownership. Browser coordinates and drawing code are disposable.

## State machines

**Job:** `BRIEF → SETUP → LIVE_EVENT → DEBRIEF → RESTART`. The deadline alone transitions SETUP to LIVE_EVENT. The live clock transitions to DEBRIEF. Reset may occur at any point.

**Player:** `FREE ↔ CARRYING`. FREE can interact with nearest item, powered equipment, or breaker. CARRYING continuously binds one item to the player and drops it freely. Future states: PUSHING, TWO_PERSON_CARRY, OPERATING_TOOL, STUNNED.

**Powered item:** `DISCONNECTED ↔ CONNECTED`; if summed circuit load exceeds capacity, circuit becomes `TRIPPED`, all devices disconnect, and breaker interaction returns it to `READY`. The prototype intentionally makes decorative lights incompatible with sound on the only circuit, forcing prioritization.

**Requirement:** derived continuously from world state; verification is a separate irreversible “check performed” flag. Moving items after verification can invalidate physical requirements while leaving proof that a check occurred; future versions should timestamp and stale the verification.

## Milestones

1. **M0 — Loop proof (current):** one job, deadline, placement, constrained power, checklist, live event, debrief.
2. **M1 — Legibility pass (2–4 days):** contextual outlines, collision/route blocking, audio, warnings, pause, tuned pacing, test coverage.
3. **M2 — Systems proof (1–2 weeks):** second job variant, rain/wind, fragile props, dolly, sequential dependencies, simple scoring and local save.
4. **M3 — Co-op graybox in Unity (3–5 weeks):** two players, shared carry, host authority, one migrated wedding venue, join/reconnect test.
5. **M4 — Vertical slice (8–12 weeks):** 2–5 players, three polished job families, progression stub, art/audio target, Steam playtest build.

## Decisions, risks, and open questions

Decisions: the event always starts; success is graded; the first power puzzle presents a meaningful tradeoff; free placement coexists with client zones; the first prototype is solo and dependency-free; Rookery County is the shared fiction.

Risks: moving many similar props could feel like chores; free placement may be fiddly; physics/netcode can make teamwork unfair; too many simultaneous systems may obscure causality; live-event NPCs can be expensive; progression could incentivize grinding. Mitigations include small counts, forgiving snap/outline feedback, discrete authority, staged system introductions, cheap crowd LOD, and contract variety over stat inflation.

Open questions to test, not debate abstractly:

- Is three minutes enough for a first run and still replayable on mastery?
- Does free placement feel expressive or merely imprecise?
- Should verification lock a requirement snapshot, or can later changes invalidate it?
- How much setup should remain possible once guests arrive?
- Which first shared-carry object creates coordination without griefing?
- Does the power tradeoff read as a deliberate choice before the first overload?
